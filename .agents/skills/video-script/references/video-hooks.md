# Video Hooks: General Architectures and Selection

**Research last checked: September 29, 2026. Generalized: October 1, 2026.** This is a topic-independent reference for educational videos, demonstrations, explainers, stories, and product walkthroughs. It has no active video, product, dataset, or incident. Infer the audience, subject, evidence, and payoff from the current request each time the skill runs.

The architectures are an editorial synthesis of supported attention principles, not experimentally ranked formulas. The evidence below primarily concerns organic YouTube, with learning and advertising findings labeled separately. Platform findings have their own scope; neither the examples nor a creator's preferred timings establish universal retention rules.

Read the selection guidance and relevant architectures before drafting. Consult the evidence table when explaining a mechanism. Refresh date-sensitive guidance when it affects a request; the generalization date does not mean the underlying research was rechecked.

## What the hook must accomplish

Give the intended viewer a specific reason to continue: recognize a relevant situation or result, understand the question or gap, and anticipate a useful payoff. Match the supplied title and thumbnail promise. Introduce enough context for the missing information to matter, while leaving a worthwhile demonstration or explanation for the body.

A common problem is a strong starting point when the audience recognizes it. Other topics may suit discovery, proof, a question, contrast, a story, or a direct how-to promise. Do not force every subject into a frustration narrative or assume a developer audience. Use the audience named or implied in the active brief.

For Lazar, apply his production preference: **20–30-second hook → existing 2–3-second MOGRT title card without narration → body**, unless he requests another format. Earn relevance immediately and build interest across the full opening. At 150 WPM, allow roughly 50–75 spoken words before subtracting time for deliberate pauses. For other creators, use their brief. Keep creator preferences separate from the general architectures.

## Choose, or offer concrete choices

1. Identify the viewer's goal, prior knowledge, reason to click, and the outcome the body can actually deliver.
2. Identify the strongest available opening material: a recognizable obstacle, a demonstrated result, an answerable question, a supported correction, a real turning point, a useful comparison, a small puzzle, or a clear task.
3. Compare genuinely different suitable architectures. Reject candidates that require invented stakes, proof, personal experience, numbers, misconceptions, or outcomes.
4. Select the strongest fit and state one brief reason with the draft when the angle is clear. Do not add a new approval stop to authorized drafting.
5. When the user wants to explore, motivation is unclear, or materially different angles are similarly strong, offer a choice. Put the recommendation first and supply an opener tailored to the active subject for every offered type. Shortlist suitable types normally; show all eight when asked to compare the entire taxonomy. Never ask the user to choose among labels alone.

When offering Lazar complete alternatives, develop each candidate to 20–30 seconds and calculate its actual words and pauses. The illustrations below are short opening seeds, not complete timed hooks or reusable claims. Expand from the current brief rather than copying a seed and padding it. If asking for a choice, wait before committing the dependent narration; continue independent body preparation when useful.

## Eight general architectures

Bracketed fields in the adaptable openers are semantic slots. Replace them with details from the active brief; they are not spoken text. The illustrations use different hypothetical subjects to show how each structure works. Their situations are not facts about Lazar or instructions for future videos.

### 1. Recognized problem → concrete payoff

**Structure:** familiar situation → meaningful obstacle or consequence → credible route → useful outcome.

**Choose when:** the viewer experiences a recurring frustration, limitation, or decision and needs a reason to care before learning a method or feature. Prefer this when a recognizable problem fits; do not make it mandatory.

**Adaptable opener:** “You’re trying to [goal], but [recognizable obstacle] keeps getting in the way. [Why it matters]. I’ll show you [method or demonstration] so you can [specific outcome].”

**Illustration — organizing reference material:** “You saved the reference somewhere. Now you’re searching folders instead of working on the project. I’ll show you a way to organize those references so you can find what you need when you need it.”

**Avoid:** invented suffering, blaming every viewer, inflated consequences, and promising a solved problem when the body only investigates it.

### 2. Result/proof first → how

**Structure:** legible result or artifact → relevant significance → question about the method → demonstration.

**Choose when:** a real output, transformation, finding, or comparison is immediately understandable and the process is worth learning. The result may be a useful diagnosis or distinction, not necessarily a success story.

**Adaptable opener:** “[Show the verified result]. [Explain what makes it useful or surprising]. I’ll walk you through [process] and [important constraint] so you can understand how to get or assess [outcome].”

**Illustration — editing:** “These two clips use the same source footage, but the second makes the explanation easier to follow. I’ll show you the changes in the edit and why I made them.”

**Avoid:** fake before/after evidence, unsupported numbers, unreadable proof, or revealing the entire method and then replaying it without adding value.

### 3. Specific question → investigation or explanation

**Structure:** answerable question → enough context to make it meaningful → evidence or method that will answer it.

**Choose when:** one uncertainty organizes the lesson, comparison, or investigation. It can concern how something works, why an outcome occurred, or which option fits a situation. A valid answer can include limits or remaining uncertainty.

**Adaptable opener:** “Why does [specific outcome] happen even when [apparently conflicting condition]? I’ll [test, inspect, or explain] [relevant evidence] to find out [bounded question].”

**Illustration — cooking experiment:** “Why do these two batches brown differently if they use the same recipe? I’ll compare how I prepared and baked them to see which differences could explain the result.”

**Avoid:** vague secrets, unrelated open loops, an unexplained question for novices, or promising causality that the available evidence cannot establish.

### 4. Assumption correction → demonstration

**Structure:** recognizable interpretation → precise supported correction → practical consequence → demonstration.

**Choose when:** a consequential misconception is established in the brief or supporting evidence. If its prevalence is unknown, use a conditional interpretation rather than attributing it to everyone.

**Adaptable opener:** “If you’re assuming [interpretation], [specific correction or missing condition] changes the answer. I’ll show you [evidence or example] and how that affects [practical decision].”

**Illustration — interface design:** “If you’re choosing colors by how they look on their own, you’re missing how they work together. I’ll compare the same button against different backgrounds so you can judge the combinations.”

**Avoid:** strawmen, “everything you know is wrong,” fabricated consensus, false uniqueness, and corrections that depend on changing a term's meaning.

### 5. Mini story → unresolved next step

**Structure:** person with a goal → obstacle → relevant turning point → next step the body follows.

**Choose when:** an actual experiment, journey, incident, or creative process provides useful narrative momentum. Clearly framed hypothetical stories can work too. The character and goal should be understandable without lengthy backstory.

**Adaptable opener:** “[Person] wanted [goal]. Then [obstacle]. When [relevant discovery or turning point], the next question was [unresolved step]. I’ll show you what happened next and [lesson or method].”

**Illustration — making a presentation:** “I had the slides ready, but the rehearsal showed that the audience would need too much background before the main point made sense. So I tried a different opening. Let me show you what I changed.”

**Avoid:** invented first-person experiences, fictional customer incidents presented as real, backstory before the goal, and a story whose resolution has little to do with the lesson.

### 6. Contrast → useful distinction

**Structure:** relevant option or view A → option or view B → meaningful difference → criterion for choosing or using them.

**Choose when:** the video compares approaches, perspectives, workflows, or outcomes and can explain a practical tradeoff. Both sides must be represented fairly; a comparison need not establish a universal winner.

**Adaptable opener:** “[A] helps you [benefit], while [B] gives you [different benefit]. The choice depends on [relevant condition]. I’ll compare them using [example or criteria] so you can decide [specific decision].”

**Illustration — learning:** “Following a tutorial gives you a route through the material. Building your own example shows where you still need help. I’ll compare what each approach gives you and when I’d use it.”

**Avoid:** a fabricated bad baseline, unverified competitor claims, unreadable comparisons, or presenting correlation as proof of why one approach worked.

### 7. Bounded prediction/challenge → reveal

**Structure:** small understandable puzzle → invitation to predict → evidence that tests the prediction → reveal and explanation.

**Choose when:** the audience has enough context to make a meaningful prediction. Examples include code behavior, a visible design choice, an experiment, or a troubleshooting decision. Keep the task bounded and the eventual answer useful.

**Adaptable opener:** “Given [small example and necessary context], what do you expect [outcome] to be? [Identify the relevant uncertainty]. I’ll [run or demonstrate the test], then explain [why or when it behaves that way].”

**Illustration — programming:** “These two functions look like they should return the same value. Before I run them, what difference would you look for? I’ll test both and walk through the behavior that changes the result.”

**Avoid:** obscure trivia, misleading options, fake time pressure, insufficient context, or a challenge framed as settled when the result is inconclusive.

### 8. Direct useful promise → route

**Structure:** specific viewer capability → concrete example or method → important scope → first step.

**Choose when:** intent is already clear, such as a how-to search or a returning audience that understands the need. Lead with what the viewer can do instead of creating artificial suspense.

**Adaptable opener:** “You can [specific useful task] with [method or tool]. I’ll show you [concrete scope], including [important constraint], so you can [usable outcome]. We’ll start with [necessary first step].”

**Illustration — photography:** “I’ll show you how to set up a simple product photo beside a window, using a reflector and the camera you already have. We’ll start with the light, then adjust the setup as we take the shot.”

**Avoid:** an agenda list, vague benefits, a slogan, and promising an outcome outside the method's demonstrated scope.

## Maintain interest across the bumper

Establish relevance, sharpen the gap or useful goal, then supply concrete detail and a credible payoff. This is a flexible planning sequence, not a required second-by-second formula. Each part of the opening should add information.

End on a complete thought that leads to the promised content. For Lazar, use the existing MOGRT for 2–3 seconds with exact labels and no narration; resolve AUDIO to silence or a known existing sting. Do not restart with another greeting, agenda, or subscription request afterward.

Choose the first body beat from the active subject. A demonstration may need a brief setup; a comparison may need a shared baseline; a story may resume at its turning point; an explanation may start with a concrete example. Supply prerequisite context where needed, then begin delivering the hook's promise. Do not impose app navigation, telemetry inspection, or any other previous video's workflow on an unrelated topic.

Use visual progression to clarify the current idea. A meaningful question, readable result, or changed piece of evidence can justify a new view or overlay. A fixed cut interval or decorative interruption is not an established requirement for comprehension or retention. Leave treatments to the editor and keep evidence legible.

## Evidence and its scope

The table separates platform guidance, experiments, observational studies, theory, and advertising research. Evidence for attention or learning is not automatically evidence for voluntary viewing; ad recall is not organic YouTube audience retention. The hook taxonomy and its exact wording remain editorial choices to test with the intended audience.

Full-text access varied: use the identified primary abstracts, author results, or institutional records within their stated scope rather than inventing unavailable methods. No source establishes a guaranteed retention gain for the adaptable openers or illustrations.

## Review the opening after publication

Record the hook's architecture and promise, exact opening duration, bumper start/end, first promised demonstration or explanation, and first substantive explanation. Compare videos with similar topic intent, duration, packaging, traffic source, and audience mix.

- Inspect YouTube's 30-second intro percentage **and** the curve through the first minute. A strong point at 30 seconds can still be followed by a drop at the bumper or delayed demonstration.
- Look immediately before and after the title card, and at the first promised body beat. A local dip identifies where to inspect; it does not by itself prove which element caused abandonment.
- Distinguish new/returning, subscriber/non-subscriber, and paid/organic traffic where data is available. Read viewer questions and confusion alongside quantitative retention.
- Use the channel's own comparable-video baseline. Do not invent a “must hit 70%/80%” target, claim statistical confidence from total views, or equate a retention-curve difference with unique-viewer survival. Curve changes are descriptive percentage-point differences and can include skipping or rewatching.
- Allow the platform's usual processing delay and review patterns across releases. Topic, packaging, and audience changes can confound comparisons between different videos. If testing opening edits with volunteers, keep the remaining video/packaging stable and randomize versions where practical; that feedback is still different from a public platform experiment.

Keep a hook that makes the promised lesson worth watching and delivers it honestly. Revise the premise when recognition or payoff fails, the bridge when the bumper breaks momentum, and the body when the promised evidence arrives too late. Do not make surface wording changes the only response to a rejected hook.
